    Redistributing Ilm-NMR-P31: upstream licensing and the "link, don't host" proposal

    This is not legal advice, and I am not a lawyer. It is a research memo describing how the relevant law is generally understood, written so that the maintainer can put sharp questions to KIT's legal office instead of vague ones. Every legal source cited below was fetched and read; where I could not verify something I say so inline, marked [UNVERIFIED]. Do not act on this without the legal office signing off on the two or three points flagged as decisive.

    The two answers, up front

    Q1 — "The authors already published it, so it's more or less public. Can we take it?"

    No, that reasoning is wrong — but the conclusion is closer to "yes, mostly" than you'd expect, for entirely different reasons.

    Publishing something under CC-BY-4.0 cannot grant rights the publisher never held. The licence says so itself: the grant is limited to rights "that the Licensor has authority to license" (CC BY 4.0 §1(7)), and §5(a) expressly disclaims any warranty of title or non-infringement. So the CC-BY tag on the Zenodo record is not a shield. That is the nemo dat point and it is correct.

    But "is it protected at all?" is the question that actually decides this, and the answer splits the dataset cleanly in two:

    Source block 3D structures EU database right Status
    HBP — Tebby, CRC Press, 1991 6,608 Term expired 31 Dec 2012 (§137g(2) UrhG); maker is US, likely outside Art. 11 Very likely clean
    Lan1/2/3 — Landolt-Börnstein III/40H, Springer, 2013/14 5,114 Term runs to ~2029; German maker; investment is in obtaining, not creating This is the exposure
    NMRexp (journal-scraped) 0 — n/a

    The individual chemical-shift values are not protected as such anywhere — not in Germany, not in the US. What is potentially protected is the compilation. And the compilation right on the larger of the two blocks (56% of your 3D structures) ran out thirteen years ago.

    So the honest position is: the Landolt-Börnstein-derived 5,114 structures are the problem, and they are separable. Your source column already distinguishes them. See the middle path below.

    Q2 — "What if we fetch from Zenodo instead of hosting it ourselves?"

    Legally it does not help you, and it is the wrong counterparty. Engineering-wise it is a structural change, not a small one, and it destroys the main thing the project is for.

    Not hosting avoids "extraction". It does not avoid "re-utilisation", which is defined as "any form of making available to the public ... by ... on-line or other forms of transmission" (Art. 7(2)(b), Directive 96/9/EC). The CJEU in Innoweb held that an operator who merely publishes a retrieval mechanism re-utilises the database — the mechanism "comes close to the manufacture of a parasitical competing product ... albeit without copying the information stored in the database concerned" (C‑202/12, para 48). CV-Online puts it even more bluntly: hyperlinks are "merely external manifestations, of secondary importance, of that extraction and that re-utilisation" (C‑762/19, para 37).

    And the deeper problem: the party you'd be linking to is not the party with the complaint. The Ilm-NMR authors already consented — their CC-BY grant over their own layer is valid. Springer never consented to anything and is not on the other end of that link. Routing through Zenodo changes nothing about Springer's position.

    On the engineering side: connectors.py already contains a ZenodoSource, but it is imported only by the build-time create_graph_datasets__*.py scripts. Nothing in the load path touches it. Wiring it into main.py:ensure_dataset() means a new source abstraction, a version-aware cache (the current one is keyed on {name}.{typ} with no version and no checksum), a pinned client-side processing pipeline, a 155 MB download per user instead of a gzipped .mpack, and the loss of offline use. Details below.

    1. The analysis: what is actually protected

    1.1 The individual shift values — not protected, clearly

    Three independent confirmations, and this one is not close:

    • Recital 45, Directive 96/9/EC: "the right to prevent unauthorized extraction and/or re-utilization does not in any way constitute an extension of copyright protection to mere facts or data."
    • Recital 46: the right "should not give rise to the creation of a new right in the works, data or materials themselves."
    • Art. 3(2): "The copyright protection of databases ... shall not extend to their contents."

    German law is the same: §2(2) UrhG protects only a persönliche geistige Schöpfung, which a measured ppm value is not. In the US, Feist reaches the identical result by a different route: "No one may claim originality as to facts ... The distinction is one between creation and discovery: The first person to find and report a particular fact has not created the fact" (499 U.S. 340).

    So nobody can stop you from using a ³¹P shift value. The fight, if there is one, is over taking the collection.

    1.2 Do the books count as "databases" at all? — Yes

    This was the right question to ask, and the answer is yes, printed reference works qualify. Art. 1(2) defines a database as a collection "individually accessible by electronic or other means" — the "or other means" is doing exactly this work. The CJEU confirmed the breadth in Fixtures Marketing v OPAP: the term "refers to any collection of works, data or other materials, separable from one another without the value of their contents being affected, including a method or system of some sort for the retrieval of each of its constituent materials" (C‑444/02, summary point 1).

    A ³¹P NMR reference table indexed by compound is squarely inside that. Germany transposes it identically in §87a(1) UrhG ("einzeln mit Hilfe elektronischer Mittel oder auf andere Weise zugänglich").

    1.3 Is there "substantial investment"? — Yes, and this cuts against you

    There's a well-known escape hatch here that does not apply. In BHB and OPAP the CJEU held that investment in creating data doesn't count — only investment in obtaining it, i.e. "the resources used to seek out existing independent materials and collect them in the database" (C‑203/02, summary point 1). That is why the horseracing board lost: they created the fixture data themselves.

    Springer and Tebby did the opposite. They did not measure a single shift; they trawled thousands of primary papers (the derived dataset cites 3,648 references at v1, 6,853 at v3) and collected the values. That is the paradigm case of "obtaining." So unlike BHB, the substantial-investment limb is likely satisfied for both books. No help here.

    1.4 Term — and this is where the case actually turns

    Sui generis protection lasts 15 years, not 70. §87d UrhG: "Die Rechte des Datenbankherstellers erlöschen fünfzehn Jahre nach der Veröffentlichung der Datenbank." Art. 10(1)–(2) is the same.

    Tebby, Handbook of Phosphorus-31 NMR Data, CRC Press, 1991 — the source of 6,608 of your 11,722 3D structures:

    • §137g(2) UrhG: for databases made between 1 Jan 1983 and 31 Dec 1997, "Die Schutzfrist beginnt in diesen Fällen am 1. Januar 1998." Fifteen years from 1 Jan 1998 → expired 31 December 2012. (Art. 14(3)/(5) of the Directive is to the same effect.)
    • Independently, Art. 11 limits the right to makers who are EU nationals/residents or EU-formed companies. CRC Press is Boca Raton, Florida. Art. 11(3) allows the Council to extend protection to third countries by agreement, and Recital 56 conditions it on reciprocity. [UNVERIFIED: I could not retrieve a Council decision under Art. 11(3) covering the US. My searching surfaced only a reference to an Isle of Man arrangement, which I did not retrieve or confirm. Treat the "no US extension" point as probable but unconfirmed — the term point above is the solid one and does not depend on it.]

    Landolt-Börnstein III/40H, Springer, 2013/2014 — the source of 5,114 structures. The Ilm-NMR paper itself dates it: it digitised the Landolt-Börnstein "from 2014" (J. Cheminform. 2023, Methods; the cited reference is Gupta, Lechner, Kumar et al., vol. 40, Springer, 2013). Springer-Verlag is German, so Art. 11 is satisfied. Term runs to roughly the end of 2028 or 2029. This one is live, and 5,114 structures out of a 2,713 + 1,596 + 805 breakdown is plainly a substantial part both quantitatively (it is the bulk of those subvolumes' 3D- representable content) and qualitatively — BHB defines the qualitative test as "the scale of the investment in the obtaining, verification or presentation of the contents ... regardless of whether that subject represents a quantitatively substantial part" (C‑203/02, summary point 3).

    One more trap worth knowing: §87a(1) sentence 2 — a database "wesentlich geänderte" counts as a new database with a fresh 15-year term, if the change required substantial investment (Art. 10(3) likewise). So a revised Landolt-Börnstein subvolume would restart the clock. Worth checking whether III/40H has been revised since 2014.

    1.5 Copyright in the compilation — a separate, weaker claim

    Distinct from the database right, §4 UrhG protects a Sammelwerk where "die Auswahl oder Anordnung der Elemente eine persönliche geistige Schöpfung" is — for 70 years post mortem auctoris. But it protects only the selection or arrangement, expressly "unbeschadet eines an den einzelnen Elementen gegebenenfalls bestehenden Urheberrechts".

    The Ilm-NMR authors have a decent answer to this: per the paper's Methods, they re-drew every structure by hand in ACD/ChemSketch, generated their own SMILES, their own IDs and their own schema. They took the values, not the presentation. Whether taking the entire selection is itself an infringement of the selection is the arguable residue, and it is genuinely arguable — but it is a weaker claim than the database right and I would not expect it to be the thing that bites.

    1.6 The TDM exceptions do not cover you — this is clear

    This is the argument people reach for and it does not work.

    §44b UrhG (implementing DSM Art. 4) permits "Vervielfältigungen von rechtmäßig zugänglichen Werken für das Text und Data Mining" — reproductions only. It says nothing about distribution or making available. It also requires deletion once no longer needed ("Die Vervielfältigungen sind zu löschen, wenn sie für das Text und Data Mining nicht mehr erforderlich sind") and is subject to machine-readable opt-out (§44b(3)). §87c(1) Nr. 4 extends it to substantial parts of databases — again, reproduction only.

    §60d UrhG (DSM Art. 3) is broader for research organisations and does permit making copies available — but only "einem bestimmt abgegrenzten Kreis von Personen für deren gemeinsame wissenschaftliche Forschung" and to individual third parties for quality verification, and it must stop when the research concludes (§60d(4)).

    Neither of these authorises publishing a corpus on PyPI and a public Nextcloud to an unbounded worldwide audience. The TDM exceptions let you train a model on the data. They do not let you ship the data. ChemMatData's entire product is shipping the data.

    (Also note DSM Art. 7(1): contractual terms overriding the Art. 3 research exception are unenforceable — helpful for your own mining, irrelevant to redistribution.)

    1.7 Good-faith reliance — helps with damages, not with an injunction

    Under the database right, infringement does not require knowledge. What good faith affects is remedies, and §97 UrhG splits them cleanly. §97(1) gives removal and injunctive relief against anyone who "das Urheberrecht oder ein anderes nach diesem Gesetz geschütztes Recht widerrechtlich verletzt" — unlawfulness only, no fault required, and it extends to a first threatened infringement. §97(2) gives damages only against someone who acts "vorsätzlich oder fahrlässig". So:

    • Takedown / injunction: available against you even if you acted in perfect good faith.
    • Damages: require fault. Reliance on a facially valid CC-BY grant from a peer-reviewed J. Cheminformatics paper is decent evidence against negligence.
    • But: this memo destroys that. Once you have read an analysis saying the shifts came out of a 2014 Springer reference work with no documented permission, you have actual knowledge, and the good-faith argument for future distribution is gone. That is an unwelcome consequence of asking the question, and I'd rather say it than not.

    GS Media (C‑160/15) does make knowledge and profit-motive decisive — but it is a copyright case about communication to the public under Art. 3(1) of Directive 2001/29, not a database-right case. Do not assume its knowledge test transfers to §87b.

    1.8 The US contrast — the same act is lawful there

    Feist killed "sweat of the brow" outright: the doctrine's "most glaring" flaw was that "it extended copyright protection in a compilation beyond selection and arrangement — the compiler's original contributions — to the facts themselves," which "eschewed the most fundamental axiom of copyright law" (499 U.S. 340). US compilations get only thin protection in original selection/arrangement, per 17 U.S.C. §103(b), and there is no US sui generis database right — Congress considered database bills in the late 1990s/2000s and none passed. [UNVERIFIED: I did not retrieve legislative history for the failed database bills; the absence of a federal database right is however evident from the Feist framework and from the Directive's own Recital 56 reciprocity condition, which would be pointless if the US had an equivalent.]

    Concretely: a US-based maintainer redistributing the Ilm-NMR shift values, including the Landolt-Börnstein-derived ones, is doing something lawful. You, in Germany, hosting on a German Nextcloud, are not in the same position for the Landolt-Börnstein subset. Liability follows where you act, not where your users are — so a worldwide user base does not dilute your exposure, and your US users' lawful downloads do not launder your distribution.

    2. Question 2(a): does linking change the legal position?

    Extraction: yes, avoided. Art. 7(2)(a) requires "the permanent or temporary transfer of all or a substantial part of the contents ... to another medium". If no bytes touch your server, you don't transfer. (Note Directmedia, C‑304/07, holds that even manual on-screen consultation followed by re-keying is capable of being extraction — so this is narrower than it sounds, but the no-hosting version does escape it.)

    Re-utilisation: no, not avoided. This is where the proposal fails.

    Innoweb is the case on point, and it is close to fatal. The Court held that the operator of a dedicated meta search engine re-utilises the database, reasoning that:

    "the relevant aspect of the activity of the operator ... — that is to say, making that search engine available on the Internet — comes close to the manufacture of a parasitical competing product ..., albeit without copying the information stored in the database concerned. In view of the search options offered, such a dedicated meta search engine resembles a database, but without having any data itself." (para 48)

    And on timing — which kills the "but the user does the downloading" defence:

    "actions actually carried out by the operator ... occur prior to activities carried out by end users and before a search is made in response to a given query. Such actions consist in making a dedicated meta search engine available on the Internet..." (para 29)

    The operator's act is complete when the mechanism ships. That is pip install chem_mat_database.

    CV-Online (C‑762/19) then dismissed the "but it's just a link" framing directly: hyperlinks and meta tags "are merely external manifestations, of secondary importance, of that extraction and that re-utilisation" (para 37).

    Why Svensson doesn't rescue this. Svensson holds that "the provision on a website of clickable links to works freely available on another website does not constitute an 'act of communication to the public'" (C‑466/12, operative part). That is a copyright holding under Art. 3(1) of Directive 2001/29. It is not a holding about Art. 7 of Directive 96/9, and Innoweb — decided two months earlier — reaches the opposite result on the database right for functionally similar conduct. The two coexist because they concern different rights. Anyone telling you "linking is always fine, see Svensson" is conflating them.

    The one authority that genuinely helps you is Renckhoff, and it is worth understanding why. The Court distinguished linking from re-hosting precisely on control:

    "As regards the act of communication constituted by the posting on a website of a hyperlink ..., the preventive nature of the rights of the holder are preserved, since it is open to the author, if he no longer wishes to communicate his work on the website concerned, to remove it ..., rendering obsolete any hyperlink leading to it. However ... the posting on another website of a work gives rise to a new communication, independent of the communication initially authorised." (C‑161/17, para 44)

    That is a real difference and it is in your favour on the copyright layer: if Springer ever forced a takedown at Zenodo, a link-based loader would go dark automatically, whereas your Nextcloud copy would not. It does not, however, answer the database-right point.

    Caching on the user's machine does not change the operator's position. The user may have their own §44b defence for their own copy; you don't inherit it, and per Innoweb para 29 your act completed before theirs began.

    Shipping the processing recipe alongside makes it worse, not better. Innoweb's first limb is whether the tool "provides the end user with a search form which essentially offers the same range of functionality as ... the database site". A loader that fetches the raw Zenodo archive and applies your curation, filtering and featurisation offers materially more functionality than the source. You would be arguing that you're less of a re-utiliser than Innoweb while shipping a strictly more capable product.

    The genuinely open question

    CV-Online did not stop at "this is re-utilisation." It made harm a condition:

    re-utilisation "may be prohibited by the maker of such a database where those acts adversely affect its investment ..., namely that they constitute a risk to the possibility of redeeming that investment through the normal operation of the database in question" (operative part)

    and the Court framed the exercise as striking "a fair balance" between makers redeeming investment and "that of users and competitors ... in having access to the information ... and the possibility of creating innovative products based on that information" (para 140).

    Both Innoweb and CV-Online involved commercial products that diverted advertising revenue from a competitor. A non-commercial academic loader that sends users to Zenodo — where Springer earns nothing either way — is a long way from that fact pattern. Nobody has litigated whether the harm condition is met when the "maker" whose investment is at risk is two hops upstream of the resource being linked, and when the linker is non-commercial.

    That is the untested question, and it is the one worth putting to the legal office. My reading is that ChemMatData has a real argument on the harm limb and a poor one on the "is it re-utilisation at all" limb — which means the linking scheme buys you a defence, not safety, at a large engineering cost.

    3. Question 2(b): engineering viability

    I read main.py, cache.py, dataset.py, web.py, config.py, connectors.py and cli.py in the worktree. This is a structural change, not a small one. Six specific reasons.

    3.1 The load path has exactly one source model

    load_graph_dataset / load_smiles_dataset / load_xyz_dataset all funnel into chem_mat_data/main.py:77 ensure_dataset(), which does: local file → config.cache → web.py:NextcloudFileShare. web.py:269 construct_download_url() hard-codes a Nextcloud WebDAV path ({base_url}/public.php/dav/files/{share_token}/{file_name}), and ensure_dataset assumes one file per dataset per extension with a .gz/.zip fallback ladder. There is a FILE_SHARE_CLASS_MAP in main.py:26 but it has exactly one entry ('nextcloud'), and metadata.yml is fetched from the same share to decide what exists at all (main.py:145). A per-dataset source override is new plumbing through all of it.

    3.2 ZenodoSource exists — but only at build time

    connectors.py has ZenodoSource, FigshareSource and FileDownloadSource. Grepping for importers: every one is a chem_mat_data/scripts/create_graph_datasets__*.py plus tests/test_connectors.py. Nothing in the user-facing load path imports connectors at all. That separation is currently doing real work — it's the line between "maintainer's machine, once" and "user's machine, every time." The proposal erases it.

    ZenodoSource also isn't built for this. prepare() does tempfile.mkdtemp(), fetch() downloads the whole archive and extracts it, and cleanup() runs from __del__. It is deliberately ephemeral and has no Cache integration whatsoever. On the code as it stands, every single load re-downloads the whole archive.

    3.3 The cache cannot express versions

    cache.py:171 construct_base_name() returns f'{name}.{typ}'. That is the entire cache key. No version, no checksum, no source URL. Two different Zenodo versions of Ilm-NMR-P31 would occupy the same slot with nothing to distinguish them, and contains_dataset() (cache.py:21) checks only whether the .yml exists — so a stale v2.0 cache entry would satisfy a v3.0 request silently. Adding version-awareness means changing the key format, which invalidates every existing user's cache.

    3.4 Reproducibility: this record is a moving target

    I queried the Zenodo API. The concept record is 8260782; there are five versions in under three years:

    Record DOI Version Published Files
    8260783 10.5281/zenodo.8260783 1.0 2023-08-18 4
    15658468 10.5281/zenodo.15658468 2.0 2025-06-13 5
    17649620 10.5281/zenodo.17649620 2.1 2025-11-19 5
    17952686 10.5281/zenodo.17952686 2.2 2025-12-17 5
    20762603 10.5281/zenodo.20762603 3.0 2026-06-19 5

    The content is not stable in any sense that matters:

    • Entry count went 14,250 → 43,317; molecules 13,730 → 39,107; references 3,648 → 6,853.
    • xyz_nmr_data.zip did not exist before v2.0. The 3D geometries you want appeared in June 2025, then grew 136.4 MB → 155.1 MB by v3.0.
    • v3.0 removed the origin column and changed the SDF NMREDATA_* tags.

    So: a concept-DOI pin (10.5281/zenodo.8260782) always resolves to the newest version and would have silently swapped a 14k-molecule dataset for a 43k one under your users' feet, and would have broken any script reading origin. A versioned-DOI pin (20762603) is stable — but then you hand-re-pin on every upstream release, which is a maintenance burden you don't have today, and Zenodo depositors can restrict or withdraw old versions. Neither option is as reproducible as a frozen .mpack on your own share.

    Note also that the paper cites 10.5281/zenodo.8260783 — the versioned DOI of v1.0. The v1.0 the paper describes doesn't even contain 3D coordinates.

    3.5 It discards the entire point of the .mpack model

    The value proposition of ChemMatData is that expensive processing happens once, centrally. load_graph_dataset is ensure_dataset(...) then load_graphs(file_path) — a msgpack deserialise into arrays. Fetch-at-load-time means every user instead:

    • downloads 155 MB (xyz_nmr_data.zip at v3.0) rather than a gzipped .mpack;
    • extracts it;
    • re-runs the RDKit parsing and featurisation in processing.py (56 KB) and the XyzDataset / GraphDataset path in dataset.py (53 KB);
    • and gets results that depend on their RDKit/OpenBabel versions.

    That last one is the serious one. Today an RDKit change is your problem, absorbed once at build time. Under the proposal it becomes a silent per-user data divergence — the same load_xyz_dataset('ilm_nmr_p31') call returning different featurisations on different machines. To avoid that you'd have to pin and ship a reproducible processing environment, which is a much bigger commitment than a download path.

    3.6 Offline use, rate limits, failure modes

    Offline use currently works: main.py:112 returns early if the file is on disk, and main.py:124 consults the cache before any network call. A Zenodo-backed path is offline- capable only to the extent you build a cache for it — i.e. you re-implement the thing you already have, against a second backend.

    You also inherit a dependency you don't control: Zenodo availability, Zenodo's rate limits (a CI matrix or a lecture hall behind one NAT is a realistic trigger) [UNVERIFIED: I did not fetch Zenodo's current published rate-limit figures, so treat the severity as unquantified — the dependency itself is certain, the thresholds are not], and per-file URLs that change shape between record versions. ZenodoSource.fetch() currently does a bare requests.get(..., stream=True) with raise_for_status() and no retry, no backoff, no resume, no checksum verification — for a 155 MB download.

    Verdict

    Small change: adding a source: field to metadata.yml and a build-time connector. You already have that. Structural change: making the user's load path fetch from arbitrary third-party repositories, with a version-aware cache, a pinned client-side processing environment, and a second failure domain. That is the one being proposed, and it buys a legal defence that Innoweb and CV-Online suggest is weak anyway.

    4. The option nobody has proposed

    The framing so far has been all-or-nothing: ship everything, or ship nothing, or link. The term analysis in §1.4 opens a fourth door.

    Ship the HBP-derived subset. Hold back the Landolt-Börnstein-derived subset until 2029.

    • HBP (Tebby / CRC Press, 1991): 6,608 structures — 56% of your 3D data. The German database right expired 31 Dec 2012 under §137g(2) UrhG. The values were never protected as such. The compilation-copyright claim on selection/arrangement is weak given that the Ilm-NMR authors re-drew every structure and built their own schema. This block is on firm ground.
    • Lan1/2/3 (Landolt-Börnstein / Springer, 2013/14): 5,114 structures. Live database right until ~2029. This is the block to hold, or to get permission for.

    You said the 3D subset "is 100% book-derived and cannot be filtered clean" — which is true as stated, but the two books are not in the same legal position, and the dataset distinguishes them. Your own count is broken out by source (HBP 6,608; Lan1 2,713; Lan2 1,596; Lan3 805), so the filter is a source column predicate, not a research project.

    A 6,608-structure ³¹P dataset with DFT geometries is still a genuinely useful public resource, and it ships now instead of in three years.

    In parallel, ask Springer. A written permission request to Springer Nature for use of Landolt-Börnstein III/40H-derived shift values in a non-commercial academic ML dataset is not an outlandish ask, it costs one email, and a "yes" resolves the whole question. It is also worth telling the Ilm-NMR authors what you found — they may already hold a permission that simply isn't documented in the paper, which would collapse this entire analysis.

    5. A general rule for the collection

    Proposed addition to .claude/skills/dataset-extraction/references/license.md. It extends the existing "Trap 1 — the upstream database" section, which already gets the principle right but stops one step short.

    5.1 The rule

    A dataset's licence describes the last link in its provenance chain. Your right to redistribute is determined by the most restrictive link, not the last one.

    Every compiled dataset has a chain: primary measurement → some compilation → possibly another compilation → the record you found. A CC-BY tag on the final record binds only the rights that depositor actually held. CC BY 4.0 §1(7) limits the grant to rights "that the Licensor has authority to license", and §5(a) disclaims any warranty of title — the licence itself tells you it is not a guarantee.

    5.2 The classification

    Add a BLOCK tier. The current table has only PASS and ESCALATE, which forces genuinely unshippable cases into the same bucket as merely-awkward ones.

    Verdict Trigger
    BLOCK Data digitised or scraped from a commercially sold reference work, database or journal corpus, where (a) no publisher permission is documented, and (b) the source was published within the last 15 years, and (c) the source publisher is EU/EEA-based. All three must hold.
    ESCALATE Any two of (a)/(b)/(c). Also: everything already in the existing ESCALATE row (NC, SA, copyleft, bespoke terms, no licence stated). Also: an upstream reference work more than 15 years old where the selection is being taken wholesale rather than the values.
    PASS Upstream is primary literature, a public database with permissive terms, the depositor's own measurements or computations, or a compilation whose sui generis term has expired and whose values are being taken rather than its arrangement.

    5.3 Three new manifest fields

    The existing upstream_database / upstream_terms_url / upstream_license fields cannot express any of the above. Add:

    {
  "upstream_publication_year": 2014,
  "upstream_publisher_jurisdiction": "DE",
  "upstream_is_commercial_product": true
}

    These are cheap to establish (they're on the book's copyright page) and they mechanise the BLOCK/ESCALATE decision instead of leaving it to judgement at the point where, as license.md already observes, "an ambiguous license gets read charitably."

    5.4 Two rules of thumb worth stating flatly

    • "It's already public" is never an answer. Publication by a third party is not a grant. Nor is "everyone else uses this dataset." The existing file says this about missing licences; it should say it about upstream rights too, which is the harder case.
    • Linking is not a workaround. If a dataset would be BLOCKed when hosted, it is BLOCKed when fetched at load time. Innoweb para 48 and CV-Online para 37 are the reason. Record this so that the next person to have this idea finds the answer instead of re-deriving it.

    5.5 One structural gap

    metadata.yml has 46 datasets and no licence field at all. The keys in use are: compounds, description, raw, tags, target_type, targets, verbose, sources, target_descriptions, full, notes, category, min_version. sources is free text and appears on 36 of 46.

    So the skill's licence gate produces a finding that is then discarded — it lives in .dataset_work/<slug>/manifest.yml and never reaches the shipped metadata or the user. A license: block in metadata.yml, surfaced by cmdata info, would mean attribution obligations actually travel with the data (which CC-BY §3(a) requires of you when you Share) and that any future problem is auditable across the collection rather than one dataset at a time. That is a small change with a large payoff, and it is worth doing regardless of how Ilm-NMR-P31 is resolved.

    6. What to ask the legal office

    Not "is this OK". These:

    1. Term and beneficiary. Do you agree the Tebby/CRC 1991 handbook's sui generis right expired 31 Dec 2012 under §137g(2) UrhG, and that as a US-made database it likely never attracted the right under §87e-relevant Art. 11 in the first place? If so, is redistributing the 6,608 HBP-derived structures acceptable, given that the shift values themselves are unprotected facts?
    2. The live block. For the 5,114 Landolt-Börnstein-derived structures (Springer, 2013/14): do you regard that as a substantial part of the III/40H subvolumes under §87b? And has III/40H been substantially revised since 2014 in a way that would restart the term under §87a(1) sentence 2?
    3. Compilation copyright. Independently of the database right — the Ilm-NMR authors re-drew all structures and built their own schema, taking values but not arrangement. Is that sufficient to avoid §4 UrhG Sammelwerk copyright, which would otherwise run for 70 years p.m.a. and would outlast the database right by decades?
    4. Downstream position. ChemMatData relies on a CC-BY-4.0 grant from a peer-reviewed publication. Given CC BY §1(7) ("rights ... that the Licensor has authority to license") and §5(a)'s disclaimer of title, what is KIT's exposure if the grant is partly invalid — and does the analysis change now that we have actual knowledge of the provenance?
    5. The linking question. Does Innoweb (C‑202/12, esp. para 48) combined with CV-Online (C‑762/19, para 37) mean that shipping a client that fetches from Zenodo at load time is "re-utilisation" under §87b, notwithstanding that no data sits on our server? And does CV-Online's harm-to-investment condition offer a real defence for a non-commercial academic tool where the upstream maker is two hops removed?
    6. Institutional posture. Does KIT want a standing policy for this class of case, given it will recur across the collection? Specifically: is the BLOCK criterion in §5.2 above (commercial + within 15 years + EU publisher, no permission) one legal is comfortable endorsing as an automated gate?
    7. Practical. Is there an institutional route for a permission request to Springer Nature for Landolt-Börnstein-derived values in a non-commercial ML dataset, and would legal prefer to send it?

    7. What I could not establish

    • Whether any Council agreement under Art. 11(3) of Directive 96/9/EC extends the sui generis right to US-made databases. Searching surfaced a reference to an Isle of Man arrangement which I did not retrieve. The Tebby conclusion does not depend on this — the term expiry under §137g(2) is independently decisive — but the Art. 11 argument is unconfirmed.
    • Whether Landolt-Börnstein III/40H has been substantially revised since 2014. If it has, §87a(1) sentence 2 restarts a fresh 15-year term and pushes the block past 2029. Checkable from Springer's catalogue; I did not.
    • The actual licence terms under which the Ilm-NMR authors obtained the Landolt-Börnstein and CRC PDFs. The paper says both "are available as PDF documents and could thus partially be read by machines," which implies institutional subscription access. Springer and CRC subscription agreements routinely contain TDM and redistribution clauses that would govern independently of copyright — a contractual restriction that no statutory analysis above touches. I could not see those agreements. This may matter more than everything above, because contract can bind where copyright does not.
    • Whether the Ilm-NMR authors in fact hold permission. Absent from the paper, but absence of documentation is not documentation of absence. Worth one email.
    • German case law on printed reference works as Datenbanken under §87a. I relied on Art. 1(2)'s "or other means" and the CJEU's broad definition in OPAP; I did not retrieve a German decision squarely on a printed scientific reference table.
    • Zenodo's current published rate limits. The runtime dependency is certain; the thresholds are unquantified here.
    • No US case law is cited beyond Feist. I did not attempt to find US authority on scientific-data compilations specifically, and the "no federal database right" statement rests on the Feist framework rather than on retrieved legislative history.
